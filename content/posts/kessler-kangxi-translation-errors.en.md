The Institute of Qing History at Renmin University of China did something pretty absurd: when translating someone else's book, it gave the book a new main title to play up the "High Qing golden age." The American sinologist Lawrence Kessler wrote *K'ang-hsi and the Consolidation of Ch'ing Rule, 1661–1684*. Translated directly, that's 《康熙与清朝统治的巩固》. But when Dong Jianzhong (董建中), an associate professor at the Institute, translated it, he added the main title 盛世的奠基, "The Foundations of a Golden Age." Now some people refer to the book by this made-up title.

<!--more-->

The translation errors are also appallingly numerous. If anything, many of them look deliberate.

Below, each English passage is Kessler's original, followed by Dong's Chinese rendering and a literal English back-translation of it, so readers who don't read Chinese can see what changed.

## 1. "Racial" becomes "extreme"

Kessler's original:

> Racial animosity toward the alien dynasty

Dong's translation:

> 针对异族王朝的极端敌意
>
> *(back-translation: "**extreme** animosity toward the alien dynasty")*

"Racial" was swapped for "extreme," as if *racial* had been read as *radical*. Kessler is stressing ethnic hostility between Manchus and Han Chinese. The translation strips out the ethnic dimension entirely and adds an intensity the original never claimed.

## 2. "Failed to strike a chord" becomes "struck a chord"

Kessler's original:

> I was struck with the apparent failure of this challenge to Manchu rule to strike a responsive chord among Chinese patriots. Racial animosity toward the alien dynasty had been common in the first few decades after the conquest

Dong's translation:

> 我在考察三藩之乱（1673—1681）时发现，尽管这一对满人统治的挑战明显失败，然而它能引发汉人的共鸣，我对此产生了强烈的兴趣。
>
> *(back-translation: "When studying the Revolt of the Three Feudatories (1673–1681), I found that **although this challenge to Manchu rule clearly failed, it was able to strike a chord with the Han Chinese**, and this aroused my strong interest.")*

Dong dropped the word "patriots" altogether. And I suspect Professor Dong simply didn't follow the logic of the sentence. Kessler is saying the Revolt of the Three Feudatories **failed** to win any response from Chinese patriots. Dong's version says the revolt failed militarily but **did** win the sympathy of the Han Chinese. That's not a matter of interpretation; it's just translating the sentence backwards.

## 3. "Had begun to accommodate" becomes "had long since adapted"

Kessler's original:

> We know from Franz Michael's pioneering work, *The Origin of Manchu Rule in China* (1942), that rulers of the Manchu state had begun to accommodate themselves to Chinese political and social traditions long before they came through the Great Wall and conquered China

Dong's translation:

> 我们从梅谷开创性的著作《满人统治中国的起源》（1942）可以知道，满洲政权的统治者在突破长城、入主中原之前，早已适应了汉人的政治和社会传统。
>
> *(back-translation: "We know from Michael's pioneering work *The Origin of Manchu Rule over China* (1942) that before breaking through the Great Wall and **coming to rule the Central Plains**, the rulers of the Manchu state **had long since adapted** to Han political and social traditions.")*

The original says "came through the Great Wall and conquered China." Dong renders "conquered China" as 入主中原, "came to rule the Central Plains," a stock phrase in Chinese historiography for a dynasty taking over the realm. It describes a change of rulers, not a conquest, so it softens the fact that this was a conquest.

On top of that, Kessler says the Manchu rulers had **begun** to accommodate themselves to Chinese traditions, an ongoing process. The translation says they had **already adapted**, turning a process that was still underway into a finished one.

And that's just the first three pages. There are 271 more to go.
